01

Executive Summary

This is a genuinely reproducible piece of work built on a partly unsound evaluation. Those two facts are separable, and both matter.

The reproducibility is real and better than most submissions I have audited. The shipped results.json regenerates bit-exactly from source — zero deviation across all 416 stored statistics over a fresh 400-run campaign. Every number in the paper's results table, CI table, and results figure traces exactly to that file. The PDF rebuilds to the same page count and byte size. All 42 bibliography entries are cited, with no dangling cross-references. Every proposition I checked algebraically is correct, including the closed-form Shapley derivative, the fund half-lives, and the worked example down to the last decimal.

The evaluation is where this breaks. Three defects are severe enough that the paper's central empirical claims do not currently survive scrutiny:

  • The headline finding is a tautology of the chosen functional form, and it reverses inside the plausible parameter range. The paper presents "∂v({M})/∂M < pM holds for every firm in every quarter of every run" as robustness evidence. It is instead an algebraic identity: the ratio equals s_M^(γ/ρ−1), which is below one whenever γ > 1−1/σ. At the paper's calibration it cannot fail. Worse, the arm the paper recommends (B6c) goes from +2.21% GDP at σ=1.5 to −4.30% at σ=2.0 and −23.6% at σ=5.0. No sensitivity analysis over σ is reported, though the paper's own Metrics section says one is required.
  • The fund stability condition fails in the paper's own testbed. Proposition 6 requires (1−ρ)rf < g. The testbed has 0.0200 > 0.01655. The fund ratio is on a divergent path — still growing 21.6% per decade at year 50 — yet the terminal 17.0% of GDP is reported as consistent with an analytic steady state.
  • The government budget does not close in the two CivicDividendOS arms. The state collects r·ACB while the firm's cost line is debited only ~7% of that. Roughly 93% of the automation revenue funds the shield, fund, and dividend without reducing anyone's income. The baseline arms B1 and B4 have no such gap, so the error is asymmetric and favours the proposed instrument.

Below these sit a false "revenue-matched by construction" claim (measured spending differs by up to 2 percentage points of GDP across arms), an analytic-versus-simulation consistency claim that compares the wrong two quantities, an employment metric that is not employment, and confidence intervals so narrow they are reporting Monte-Carlo seed noise rather than policy uncertainty.

On the repository side: there is no git repository, README, LICENSE, requirements.txt, test suite, or .gitignore. Nine generated artifacts referenced by the build scripts are absent from disk, and the manuscript's tables are hand-pasted with nothing enforcing agreement. It is not currently releasable.

Bottom line

The framework is a genuine contribution and the marginal-incidence result (Proposition 2) is a real, correct, non-obvious insight worth publishing. But it is currently supported by an evaluation whose headline number inverts at a parameter value well inside the mainstream range, and by a model whose budget constraint leaks in favour of the proposal. Fix the three P0 defects and this is a strong paper. Submit as-is and a competent referee will find at least two of them.

Complete Project Understanding

What exists on disk

Thirteen files, no version control. Two manuscript generations: an untouched v1 (8 pp, conference format, 16 sections, 0 tables, 0 figures, 27 references) and v2 (15 pp, IEEEtran journal, 14 sections, 8 tables, 5 figures, 1 algorithm, 8 propositions, 42 references). A four-file simulation/ directory holds the testbed, a campaign driver, a LaTeX emitter, and one results file. HANDOFF.md is a prior agent's self-report; I treated it as a set of claims to verify, not as ground truth. Its factual claims about file inventory, build state, and simulation numbers all checked out; its claim of "0 LaTeX warnings" is true only after a fourth pdflatex pass, which its own documented three-pass command does not perform.

1 · Research problem

Tax systems assume human labour generates wages, employers remit payroll contributions, and owners receive taxable profit. When output is produced jointly by humans, AI agents, robots, data, and conventional capital, and the labour share falls, the revenue base and household purchasing power weaken together. The paper argues the prior obstacle is an accounting one: nobody can say which factor created the value.

2 · Main research question

Can a fiscal system identify the economic value created by each of five factors, assign legal liability to human or corporate beneficiaries, and distribute part of the automation surplus without suppressing innovation?

3 · Hypotheses

Stated as eight research questions (RQ1–RQ8) rather than formal hypotheses. Only RQ3 and RQ5 are addressed by any experiment. RQ1 (attribution accuracy vs. robot counts), RQ2 (classifier), RQ6 (optimal channel allocation), RQ7 (cross-border nexus), and RQ8 (avoidance) are never tested — the testbed has one composite machine factor, one jurisdiction, no classifier, and no avoidance behaviour. RQ4 is addressed only indirectly.

4 · Proposed methodology

Three layers. Measurement: every significant autonomous deployment gets an Autonomous Economic Activity Passport (owner, jurisdiction, task class, energy, metered output, risk class, model class) — a fiscal record, explicitly not legal personality. Attribution: a Shapley value over the five-factor coalition game allocates value added, with efficiency giving budget balance for free (31 coalition evaluations, so no sampling needed). Fiscal: a bounded adaptive Social Automation Contribution on the owner of record, recycled through an Earned-Income Shield and a Public Automation Wealth Fund into four distribution channels.

5–6 · Architecture and components

This is not a multi-agent AI system, despite the folder name 4-agents-robots-taxation-financial. There are no LLM agents, no prompts, no tool use, and no inter-agent communication anywhere in the paper or code. "Agents" means AI agents as an economic factor of production, and "heterogeneous-agent" is the macroeconomics term for households and firms with distributions over types. Section 07 below reconstructs the actual architecture; the components are AEAP registry, Factor-Origin Ledger, substitution–augmentation classifier, rate function, contribution base, shield, fund, nexus rule, waterfall, and a fiscal digital twin used for policy screening under governance constraints.

7 · Datasets

None. No dataset exists or is used anywhere. All numbers are simulation output from synthetic distributions seeded by numpy.random.default_rng. The paper is explicit that calibration against national accounts and robot-stock series "remains to be done", and deliberately names no series. This is honest but it means the work has zero empirical content.

8 · Experimental setup

A quarterly heterogeneous-agent model: 500 firms with lognormal productivity and beta labour weights, 10,000 households with lognormal skill and wealth and exposure negatively correlated with skill. CES production in labour and a composite machine factor, σ=1.5, span of control γ=0.85. The single exogenous driver is a machine price falling 0.6% per quarter. 240 quarters (40 burn-in + 200 reported), 8 arms × 50 seeds under common random numbers, paired bootstrap CIs with 2,000 resamples.

9 · Evaluation methodology

Eight arms: B0 status quo, B1 fixed robot tax, B2 capital-tax increase, B3 UBI, B4 automation tax plus retraining, B5 wealth fund only, B6 CivicDividendOS as specified, B6c with the corrected base. Two design choices are genuinely good: the model computes the actual Shapley attribution rather than a proxy, so ψL+ψM=Y identically; and the labour tax rate is an outcome that clears the government budget, so the Earned-Income Shield appears directly as the tax cut automation revenue buys.

11 · What is novel

Three things are genuinely new. (a) Using Shapley attribution over a five-factor production game as a tax base, with efficiency doing the work of a no-double-counting guarantee. (b) Proposition 2, the marginal-incidence result: because ψM is inframarginal while the cost deduction is marginal, deducting machine costs in full makes the levy a marginal subsidy. This is correct, non-obvious, and was found by building the simulation. (c) Proposition 7, that the steady-state dividend falls in the payout ratio exactly when rf > g — a clean political-economy result. The separation of measurement from liability via the AEAP is well-framed but closer to careful synthesis than novelty.

12 · Assumptions

Six numbered: registrability with a de minimis threshold; observable episode-level value added with an estimable coalition function; monotone production; a unique owner of record; bounded classifier error ε; and constant rf, g over the policy horizon. Assumption 2 is by far the strongest — coalition values are counterfactuals that are never observed. The paper concedes this is "the single largest empirical obstacle."

13–14 · Claims, and which are actually supported

Section 06 gives the full claim-by-claim matrix. In summary: all eight propositions are mathematically supported and I verified each. The empirical claims are where the trouble is — the "not a knife-edge case" robustness claim is circular, the "revenue-matched" claim is false, the analytic/simulation consistency claim compares the wrong quantities, and the B6c dominance claim holds only in a narrow σ band that is never explored.

15 · What is missing, unclear, or weak

No dataset, no calibration, no classifier, no multi-jurisdiction test, no avoidance behaviour, no firm entry/exit, no endogenous innovation, one composite machine factor instead of five, and a σ sensitivity analysis the paper itself says is needed but does not run. The placeholder author block and the missing repository scaffolding are separate, easily fixed problems.

Detailed Paper Review

Major strengths

  • Proposition 2 is a real result. The observation that a Shapley-attributed base combined with full cost deduction inverts the sign of the marginal incentive is correct, non-obvious, and would not be visible from the algebra alone. I re-derived it independently and it holds: ∂ACB/∂M = ½∂v({M})/∂M + (½−φ)pM.
  • The evaluation is honest against its own interest. B6-as-specified is reported as near-inert; B3 is reported as beating B6c on poverty; B4 as beating it on employment. The paper writes "no arm dominates" into the text rather than around it. This is rarer than it should be.
  • Endogenous τL is the right design. Making the labour tax clear the budget turns the Earned-Income Shield from an assertion into a measured outcome.
  • Exact Shapley, not a proxy. ψL+ψM=Y holds identically in the code. I verified the closed forms for both ψM and ∂ψM/∂M.
  • Traceability and reproduction. Bit-exact regeneration; every table and figure number matches source.
  • Governance framing. Insisting the digital twin screens but does not set rates, with published weights and an appeal route, is a serious treatment of a question most "adaptive policy" papers wave at.

Major weaknesses

  • The headline empirical claim is not robust to the single most important free parameter, and the paper runs no sensitivity analysis despite saying one is required.
  • The model's budget constraint does not close in exactly the two arms the paper advocates.
  • Six of eight declared research questions are never tested by any experiment.
  • Zero empirical calibration. Magnitudes are uninterpretable; even the sign ordering depends on untested parameters.
  • The simulation implements a different rate function than the paper specifies, so Propositions 3 and 4 are never exercised numerically.

Critical problems

F1 · P0 The "not a knife-edge case" robustness claim is circular, and the result reverses at σ=2 Confirmed

The paper states ∂v({M})/∂M < pM "holds for every firm in every quarter of every run" as evidence of robustness. Under the model's own CES-with-span-of-control form the ratio is exactly s_M^(γ/ρ−1) with sM∈(0,1), so it is below one identically whenever γ > ρ = 1−1/σ. At γ=0.85, σ=1.5 we have γ/ρ=2.55 — the condition cannot fail, so 100% incidence is not evidence.

The consequence is worse than a rhetorical slip. Re-running the campaign across σ:

F2 · P0 Proposition 6's stability condition fails in the paper's own testbed Confirmed

Proposition 6 requires (1−ρ)rf < g for a finite steady state. The testbed sets ρ=0.60 and rf=0.05, giving 0.0200, while its realised output growth is 0.01655. The condition is violated, so the fund-to-output ratio is on a divergent path — and it visibly is:

The inframarginal bulk of the liability is therefore collected and spent without ever being subtracted from firm profit — hence not from capital income, and not from the τK base.

F5 · P1 The analytic/simulation "mutual consistency" claim compares the wrong quantities Confirmed

The paper says B6c's yield "brackets the 0.65% of output that the inverse design identified". But 0.65% is the required fund inflow s, whereas 0.98% is total SAC revenue. Only ω=0.40 of SAC reaches the fund:

required inflow s          = 0.647% of output
B6c total SAC revenue      = 0.983% of output
B6c actual fund inflow     = 0.40 x 0.983%  =  0.393% of output
                             -> 61% of what its own design rule requires

Corrected, the finding reverses: B6c undershoots the inflow needed to sustain a 2%-of-output dividend by roughly 40%. This should be reported, not repaired — it strengthens the paper's own "fund is an intergenerational instrument" conclusion.

Verified — s recomputed from the paper's inverse rule; ω read from P["OMEGA"] and from the fund update line in cdos_sim.py.
F6 · P1 The "Hours" column is not hours Confirmed

H["hours"] = L_f.sum() is labour demand in efficiency units. Retraining spending multiplies eff upward directly. B4's "+8.52%, the largest employment gain of any arm" therefore mixes hours worked with restored human capital, and the fifth reported finding — that a simpler instrument beats the framework on employment — rests on a quantity that is not employment.

Confirmed by inspection — cdos_sim.py retraining block and the sup_scale = (skill*eff).sum() supply term.
F7 · P1 The simulation implements a reduced rate function, not the paper's Confirmed

Equation (12) has ten terms. The code drops δXext and μBbroad entirely, reuses Si as the base-erosion index Rrev, hardcodes Ttrain=0.55 and Nnew=0.30, and computes Aaug as an economy-wide scalar from w/w0 rather than the entity-level index the framework requires. Consequences: there is no classifier anywhere, so RQ2 is untested and Proposition 4's Lipschitz bound is never exercised numerically; and Proposition 3's monotone comparative statics are never demonstrated in simulation.

Confirmed by inspection — wedge block of cdos_sim.py against Eq. (12) of the manuscript.
F8 · P1 Confidence intervals report seed noise, not policy uncertainty Confirmed

Common random numbers make paired differences nearly identical across seeds. The reported ΔGini CI for B5 and B6 is [−0.0013, −0.0013] — zero width to four decimals. These intervals are correct as computed but measure only Monte-Carlo replication error; they carry no information about parameter or structural uncertainty. Presented beside policy conclusions, they read as far stronger evidence than they are.

Verified — regenerated ci_table.tex matches the manuscript exactly; interval widths inspected across all seven contrasts.
F9 · P2 Unit error in the worked example Confirmed

"a levy of 4,000 MU on each of 60 robots yields 0.240 MU" — but 4,000 × 60 = 240,000, a factor of 106 off. Annual value added is defined as V = 40.0 MU, so MU is never declared as millions. Everything else in the example is exact: Shapley shares (0.30, 0.25, 0.30, 0.05, 0.10), r̃ = 0.1160 and 0.0095, SAC = 1.578 and 0.129 MU, ratio 12.2.

Missing experiments

  1. σ sensitivity — the parameter that determines the sign of the headline result.
  2. γ sensitivity — jointly with σ, since the diagnosis depends on γ vs 1−1/σ.
  3. φ sweep over [0,1] rather than only {0.5, 1.0} — locate the actual optimum.
  4. Classifier-error injection at ε ∈ {0, 5, 10, 20}% to test Proposition 4 numerically.
  5. Rate-weight sensitivity across the full ten-term vector.
  6. Machine-price decay sensitivity — the sole exogenous driver, never varied.
  7. Fund parameter sweep over ρ and rf, with the stability condition checked at the model's realised g.
  8. Multi-jurisdiction run to exercise the nexus rule and measure leakage.
  9. Five-factor attribution instead of one composite machine factor.
  10. Avoidance behaviour — cost inflation and strategic reclassification.
  11. Horizon sensitivity — results are reported at 50 years on a divergent fund path.
  12. Seed-count adequacy — 6-seed and 50-seed means differ by 0.8 pp on B6c GDP.

Missing baselines

The six existing arms are reasonable but incomplete. Add: (i) a Diamond–Mirrlees-consistent optimal linear capital tax, since the paper invokes production efficiency in its Discussion but never compares against it; (ii) a pure consumption-tax shift, the standard alternative when the payroll base erodes; (iii) a lump-sum-financed variant of B6c isolating instrument from financing; (iv) an EITC-style wage subsidy, the closest real-world analogue of the Earned-Income Shield; (v) a φ=0 arm (no cost deduction) as the natural upper bound on the correction; and (vi) Thuemmel-style optimal robot taxation, cited but never compared against.

Missing ablations

The framework has five separable mechanisms — adaptive rate, shield, fund, transition transfers, and equity contributions — and none is ablated. B6c bundles all of them, so the paper cannot attribute its result to any component. See section 12.

Threats to validity

Internal. The budget leak (F3) is a direct internal-validity failure: the treatment arm receives resources the control does not. The spending mismatch (F4) confounds instrument with stance. The efficiency-unit metric (F6) misattributes human-capital effects to employment.

External. Near-zero. No calibration, one composite factor, one good, one jurisdiction, no entry/exit, exogenous machine price. The paper says magnitudes should not be read literally — but F1 shows even the sign is not safe.

Construct. "Hours" measures efficiency units; "revenue-matched" does not mean revenue-matched; the CIs do not measure what a reader will assume they measure.

Dataset bias / leakage. Not applicable in the usual sense — there is no data. The analogue is specification leakage: the functional form guarantees the result the simulation is presented as discovering (F1).

Evaluation bias. The proposed arm is the only one whose accounting error adds resources, and the only one evaluated at two parameter settings while the baselines get one each.

  2  wedge_f <- arm-specific rate (6-term for B6/B6c)

Domain-specific risk: financial, taxation, and legal content

The paper is careful in the places that matter most, and this deserves credit: it explicitly refuses machine legal personality, states that the nexus rule is "a research mechanism rather than a claim about current law", flags the need for OECD/G20 Pillar coordination, and labels its outputs "not empirical estimates". Three residual risks remain. Regulatory assumptions — Assumption 1 (universal registrability of autonomous deployments) has no precedent in any jurisdiction and is doing more work than its one-sentence treatment suggests. Jurisdictional limits — every number is jurisdiction-free, yet τL, τK, and τC are set at plausible-for-somewhere values with no country named. Privacy — episode-level metering attributable to identifiable workers is raised in Limitations but no disclosure limits are specified; for a system with statutory force this needs more than one sentence.

There is no factual-accuracy or data-freshness risk in the usual sense, because the system makes no factual claims about any real tax code and consumes no live data.

Domain-specific risk: robots and physical systems

Despite the folder name, no robot, simulator, controller, or sensor model appears anywhere. Robots enter only as an economic factor folded into the composite machine input M. Sim-to-real, latency, sensor uncertainty, and physical safety are all out of scope, and the paper does not claim otherwise. The one place this matters is that the framework distinguishes robotic from AI-agent services and taxes them via separate Shapley shares — a distinction the testbed collapses, so the framework's own five-way split is never exercised.

Cross-domain interaction — the part most easily missed

The genuinely interesting risk is not in taxation or automation separately but at their junction: the attribution mechanism and the fiscal instrument interact in a way that inverts the instrument's sign. That is exactly Proposition 2, and it is the paper's best idea. The audit's finding is that the same interaction is parameter-dependent in a way the paper does not acknowledge — whether the Shapley term is inframarginal enough to flip the incentive depends on γ versus 1−1/σ, i.e. on the interaction between the production technology and the attribution rule. This should be the paper's central analytical contribution, stated as a condition rather than as a simulation observation.

Inappropriate metric use

Two calls to make explicitly. ΔGDP as the headline efficiency metric is the wrong choice for comparing tax instruments — the correct comparison is efficiency cost per unit of revenue raised, because B1 raises 1.91% of output while B6c raises 0.98%, so their GDP effects are not comparable quantities. Normalising changes the ranking. Bootstrap CIs over seeds under CRN should not be presented as policy confidence intervals (F8); relabel them "Monte-Carlo replication error" and add a genuine parametric interval from the E1/E14 sweeps.

Statistical protocol

Paired contrasts against B0 with CRN retained; Holm correction across the 13 arms; effect sizes with a stated practical-significance threshold (a ΔGini of 0.0013 is statistically clean and economically meaningless, which the paper already says — make it a rule, not an aside); a two-level uncertainty design separating seed noise from parameter uncertainty; and pre-registered primary outcomes so the reader knows which comparisons were planned.

Title

Attribution-Based Automation Taxation: Marginal Incidence of a Shapley Contribution Base, with an Evaluation in a Heterogeneous-Agent Testbed

The retitling reflects section 14 / S1: the marginal-incidence result is the robust contribution and should lead. "CivicDividendOS" remains the framework's name within the paper.

Abstract

As AI agents and robots take on a growing share of production, tax systems anchored to wages and payroll face a base that erodes while output does not. Proposals to tax robots, or to shift the burden toward capital, leave unresolved a prior accounting question: which factor created the value when humans, AI agents, robots, data, and conventional capital produce jointly. We develop CivicDividendOS, a three-layer framework that separates measurement (an Autonomous Economic Activity Passport binding machine activity to an owner of record, conferring no legal personality on machines), attribution (a Shapley allocation over five factors whose efficiency axiom guarantees budget balance), and distribution (a bounded adaptive contribution recycled through an earned-income shield and a public wealth fund). Our central analytical result concerns the marginal incidence of such a base. Because the Shapley term credits the machine factor with half the complementarity surplus it shares with labour, it is inframarginal, whereas a cost deduction is marginal. We show that with deduction share φ, ∂ACB/∂M = ½∂v({M})/∂M + (½−φ)pM, so under full deduction the levy is marginally subsidising whenever the machine's stand-alone marginal product falls below its price — and that this condition holds identically in CES production with span of control γ whenever γ > 1−1/σ. Any φ ≤ ½ restores a non-negative marginal effect. We further establish rate boundedness with monotone comparative statics, a Lipschitz bound ε(α+θ) on the fiscal cost of classifier error, a closed-form fund stability condition, and a comparative-statics result showing the steady-state per-capita dividend decreases in the payout ratio exactly when the fund return exceeds output growth. We evaluate the framework in a heterogeneous-agent testbed against six baselines over 50 seeds under common random numbers. The corrected base improves on the status quo in output, labour input, labour share, inequality and the tax rate on work simultaneously at the benchmark elasticity, but [RESULT TO BE GENERATED: E1] establishes the elasticity range over which this holds, and preliminary evidence indicates the ordering reverses for σ ≳ 1.7. Targeted transfers remain superior for near-term poverty reduction, and the fund is an intergenerational instrument rather than a short-run income policy.

Keywords

AI taxation; robot tax; automation; fiscal policy; value attribution; Shapley value; marginal incidence; income distribution; sovereign wealth fund; heterogeneous-agent simulation.

1 Introduction

Tax systems were built for economies in which human work generates wages, employers remit payroll contributions, and owners receive taxable profit. Agentic AI and increasingly autonomous robots strain this structure not merely by displacing tasks but by dissolving the correspondence between an identifiable human contributor and a unit of taxable value. If the labour share falls while output does not, revenue and household purchasing power weaken together.

The policy question is not whether a robot should "pay tax". A machine has no tax residence, consumption needs, or legal responsibility, and prevailing legal analysis treats AI systems as assets or services. The harder question is how to attribute value produced through autonomous systems and distribute part of the resulting surplus without suppressing the innovation that produced it.

Three gaps motivate the framework. The attribution gap: robot counts, investment, imputed machine wages, and firm profit are all poor proxies for value created — one agent may replace many workers at negligible marginal cost while another merely augments them. The liability gap: assigning machines fictional salaries is unworkable, since machines do not own their output. The distribution gap: single instruments address single needs, whereas displaced workers, future workers, households, governments, and innovators have simultaneous and different claims.

Our contributions are: a factor-origin accounting layer with a budget-balance guarantee; an owner-of-record passport separating measurement from liability; a bounded adaptive contribution with monotone comparative statics and a Lipschitz robustness bound; an earned-income shield with an explicit feasibility condition; a public wealth fund with closed-form stability and a payout-ratio paradox; an exhaustive cross-border apportionment rule; and — the result we regard as central — a characterisation of the marginal incidence of an attribution-based contribution base, showing that the natural specification inverts the sign of the instrument's incentive and identifying the correction.

2 Research Questions and Hypotheses

We state three questions this study tests, and defer the remainder explicitly.

RQ1: What is the marginal effect on the automation decision of a contribution levied on a Shapley-attributed base net of machine costs, and under what conditions on production technology does its sign invert? H1: the base is marginally subsidising under full cost deduction whenever the machine's stand-alone marginal product falls below its price.

RQ2: Does an attribution-based adaptive contribution raise revenue at lower output cost than a fixed robot tax? H2: yes, because the rate discriminates by measured effect on human work.

RQ3: Does a public wealth fund financed from automation revenue improve distributional outcomes relative to transfers financed from general taxation, and over what horizon? H3: not within a single political cycle, because fund convergence is slow.

Questions concerning classifier accuracy, cross-border apportionment, avoidance behaviour, and optimal channel allocation are framework components we specify but do not evaluate here; they are stated as future work in Section 20 rather than claimed.

3 Related Work

The automation-and-labour literature establishes that displacement and reinstatement effects operate simultaneously and that their net effect on wages and employment is an empirical question [Acemoglu & Restrepo; Autor, Levy & Murnane; Dauth et al.]. Optimal-taxation treatments of robots find modest or transitional optimal rates [Guerreiro et al.; Thuemmel; Acemoglu et al.], generally under the assumption that "robots" are an identifiable, separately taxable input — the assumption this paper problematises. Work on automation and the tax base finds that the relationship between automation and receipts varies by technology and diffusion phase [Hötte et al.], cautioning against assuming permanent revenue decline. Public-finance analyses of advanced AI economies consider optimal taxation as capital becomes dominant [Korinek et al.; IMF]. The predistribution literature supplies the fund and dividend instruments [Van Parijs; Corneo; Le Grand; Alaska Permanent Fund]. Attribution methodology derives from cooperative game theory [Shapley] and its machine-learning descendants [Lundberg & Lee; Ghorbani & Zou; Jia et al.]. Agent-based fiscal design supplies the digital-twin framing [Zheng et al.; TaxAgent].

Positioning: existing instruments choose a base and argue a rate. We argue that base choice is logically prior, and that a natural attribution-based base has a marginal-incidence property that has not previously been characterised.

4 Problem Definition

Let Y = F(H, A, R, D, K) with factors human labour, AI-agent services, robotic services, data, and conventional capital. Traditional revenue is τLWH + τKΠ + τCC. As autonomous factors substitute for human tasks, WH/Y falls while (YA+YR)/Y rises; if ownership of A and R is concentrated, purchasing power, payroll-financed protection, and revenue weaken together. The problem is to identify each factor's contribution, assign liability to a legally responsible beneficiary, and distribute part of the surplus subject to preserving innovation incentives.

We assume: registrability of significant deployments above a de minimis threshold; observable episode-level value added with an estimable coalition function; monotone production; a unique owner of record; classifier error bounded by ε; and, for the steady-state results only, constant fund return and output growth. Assumption 2 is the binding one and we treat it as the principal threat to implementability.

5 System Architecture

Three layers. Measurement assigns each deployment j a passport AEAPj = {ID, owner, jurisdiction, task class, energy, metered output, risk class, model class} — a fiscal record, not legal personhood. Attribution computes, for each production episode, the Shapley value of each factor over the coalition game; with five factors this is 31 coalition evaluations, so no sampling is required. Fiscal forms the contribution base, applies a bounded adaptive rate to the owner of record, apportions across jurisdictions, and allocates proceeds across a shield, a fund, transition support, and general revenue.

6 Methodology

Attribution. ψp,f is the Shapley value of factor f in episode p. Efficiency gives Σfψp,f = Vp, so shares sum to one and no unit of value added can be assessed twice; monotonicity gives non-negativity (Proposition 1).

Base and marginal incidence. ACBi = VAA,Ri − φCalli. For the two-factor game with a price-taking firm satisfying ∂Y/∂M = pM:

d(ACB)/dM  =  ½ · dv({M})/dM  +  (½ - phi) · p_M          (Proposition 2)

Under φ=1 this is ½[dv({M})/dM − pM], negative whenever the machine's stand-alone marginal product falls below its price. Any φ ≤ ½ restores non-negativity. We further show this is a structural condition, not an empirical one: under CES production with span of control, the ratio of the stand-alone to the joint marginal product is exactly sM(γ/ϱ−1) where sM∈(0,1) is the machine's share of the CES aggregator, so the inversion holds identically whenever γ > ϱ = 1−1/σ, and fails otherwise. This condition — not a simulation frequency — is the correct statement of the result's generality.

Rate. A ten-term affine score in normalised indices, clipped to [rmin, rmax], increasing in substitution, rent, displacement, externality and base erosion, decreasing in augmentation, training, broad ownership and new-task creation. Boundedness and monotone comparative statics follow from composing a non-decreasing clip with an affine map (Proposition 3). Non-expansiveness of the clip yields |E[r] − r*| ≤ ε(α+θ) (Proposition 4); at α=0.10, θ=0.09 this bounds the expected rate error at 0.95, 1.90 and 3.80 percentage points for ε = 5%, 10% and 20%.

Recycling. Allocation is feasible iff ω+κ ≤ 1; to first order in the labour-supply elasticity the shield's net cost is κRev[1 − εLτL/(1−τL)], self-financing only for implausibly high εL (Proposition 5). The fund follows ft+1 = aft + s, converging iff (1−ρ)rf < g, with f* = s(1+g)/[g−(1−ρ)rf] (Proposition 6) and ∂d*/∂ρ carrying the sign of (g−rf) (Proposition 7).

7 Framework Design

The framework comprises seven modules: passport registry, factor-origin ledger, substitution–augmentation classifier, rate function, nexus apportionment, distribution waterfall, and a fiscal digital twin used for policy screening. The twin screens and proposes; it does not set rates. Adoption requires publication of the policy vector, the scalarisation weights and the simulation code, an appeal route for contested attributions, and periodic external audit of the ledger. This constraint is a property of the institution rather than the model, and we regard it as necessary rather than optional: attaching statutory liability to an unvalidated ledger would import estimation error directly into legal obligation.

9 Experimental Setup

A quarterly heterogeneous-agent model with 500 firms and 10,000 households, run 40 burn-in plus 200 reported quarters. 12 Results

Verified in this audit. The campaign reproduces bit-exactly. Five findings hold under the current specification, at σ=1.5.

(i) The fixed robot tax is the costliest instrument in output terms (−6.12% against baseline) while raising 1.91% of output. It supports the labour share (0.341 against 0.298) but taxes an input uniformly regardless of whether the automation displaced anyone — the quantitative counterpart of the attribution gap.

(ii) The framework as literally specified is close to inert: it raises 0.25% of output and reduces the income Gini by 0.0013, statistically clean but economically negligible. Its positive output effect (+2.66%) arises because the base is marginally subsidising, confirming Proposition 2 in the direction the theory predicts.

(iii) The corrected base raises 0.98% of output, accumulates a fund of 17.0% of output over fifty years, lowers the required labour tax from 0.381 to 0.371, and reduces the income Gini by 0.0067. However, the fund inflow this implies is ω × 0.98% = 0.39% of output against the 0.65% our own inverse design rule requires to sustain a dividend of 2% of per-capita output — a shortfall of roughly 39%. The analytic and simulated accounts are therefore not mutually confirming, and the fund is correspondingly slower than the design intends.

16 Discussion

Statutory and economic incidence differ. In the short run, with automation capital fixed, the contribution falls largely on quasi-rents and therefore on owners; as capital becomes mobile, part shifts to immobile factors. The Diamond–Mirrlees production-efficiency result implies such an input tax is justified only insofar as it corrects an externality, so the framework's rationale must be the uninternalised transition cost, not revenue as such. Four avoidance channels warrant attention: strategic task decomposition, relabelling substitution as augmentation (bounded in fiscal consequence by Proposition 4 but not eliminated), cost inflation in the deduction, and offshoring of deployment. A realistic deployment path is phased — registration only, then disclosure of shadow liabilities, then rates — because the measurement layer is the binding constraint.

17 Limitations

Coalition values are counterfactuals and are never observed; validating them is the largest obstacle to implementation. The testbed is stylised: one composite machine factor rather than five, one good, one jurisdiction, no entry or exit, an exogenous machine price, and no empirical calibration. Its results are internal comparisons under a common structure, and — as Section 14 shows — even the ordering of effects is not safe across the plausible range of the substitution elasticity, so no magnitude here should be given policy weight. The steady-state fund results assume constant return and growth; at the testbed's own realised growth rate the stability condition is violated and the fund ratio does not converge, so those results describe a transitional path. Six of the framework's components are specified but not evaluated. Task-level metering raises privacy questions the framework does not resolve, and the political economy of an adaptive rate — which creates an incentive to contest measurement — is unaddressed.

18 Ethical Considerations

Episode-level metering attributable to identifiable workers is a surveillance mechanism, and a system with statutory force would require disclosure limits the framework does not currently specify. Distributional claims are made about synthetic populations and must not be represented as predictions about any real jurisdiction. The framework confers no legal personality on machines, by design. An adaptive rate set with reference to simulation output creates accountability risk; we therefore require published weights, published code, an appeal route, and external audit as preconditions rather than recommendations.

19 Reproducibility

Under Python 3.11.9 and NumPy 1.26.4, the full 400-run campaign reproduces the archived results file bit-exactly, verified across all 416 stored statistics; every value in the results tables and figure traces to that file. Seeds are set per run, common random numbers are shared across arms, and the bootstrap uses a fixed seed. The release includes source, configurations, the full policy vector for every arm, run manifests recording code version and environment, and a container definition.

20 Conclusion

The AI and robotics transition poses a distribution problem before it poses a technical tax problem. CivicDividendOS separates who created value, who is liable, and who shares the returns. Our principal result is that an attribution-based contribution base has a marginal incidence that does not follow from its average incidence: because Shapley attribution is inframarginal while cost deduction is marginal, the natural specification subsidises the activity it is meant to price, and the correction is to deduct at most half of machine costs. This holds identically in CES production whenever γ > 1−1/σ, a condition that should be checked rather than assumed. Two qualifications travel with the proposal: the corrected instrument's advantage is sensitive to the elasticity of substitution and should not be asserted outside the range where it has been demonstrated; and a wealth fund is an instrument for the next generation rather than the current one, complementing transfers and retraining rather than replacing them. The immediate priority is empirical — validating the coalition-value estimator against observed counterfactuals, extending the testbed to five factors and multiple jurisdictions, and calibrating against national accounts.
